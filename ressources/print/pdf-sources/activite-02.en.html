1. In brief

In groups, the students describe four bones A-D, compare them to possible animals then formulate a reasoned association. In this version, cards A-D are neutral locations: the session trains the method, not the identification of species.

2. Objectives

  • Describe a shape without guessing.
  • Compare multiple visible clues.
  • Formulate a hypothesis with a degree of certainty.
  • Name the references needed to verify.

3. Expected success

Successful

Two clues support the association and one uncertainty is named.

In progress

An association is proposed with a single clue.

To resume

The choice rests on the assumed size or chance.

4. Duration and material

ContextDurationMaterial
Class45-60 minsSheet, animal cards, A-D cards, pencils.
Museum30-45 minsReplicas, educational bones or validated photographs; animal cards.

5. Quick preparation

Cut out the animal cards. Use cards A-D only as templates until the scientific corpus is integrated. Do not prepare a specific response.

“Your job is not to guess. You must defend a hypothesis and say what is missing to verify it. »

7. Questions and reminders

Observe

  • What is wide or narrow?
  • What surfaces appear articular?
  • Which view should be added?

Argue

  • Does your clue exclude an animal?
  • What other explanation is possible?
  • What measurement would help verify?

8. Responses or expected elements

No A-D attribution is expected in this release. The expected productions are verifiable descriptions, an association formulated as a hypothesis, at least two clues and a list of missing information: orientation, scale, species, provenance and comparison to a reference corpus.

9. Differentiation

NeedAdaptation
HelpReduce to two animals and provide the word bank.
Lighten the writingHave the argument recorded or dictated.
Go deeperHave the clues classified from the weakest to the strongest.

10. Museum adaptation

Only use a manipulatable object if museum rules allow it. Otherwise, use an oriented photograph, showcase or replica. Adapt the species to the corpus actually available; never let people believe that a museum necessarily owns the collections mentioned.

11. Quick assessment

Request a sentence comprising four elements: possible association, clue 1, clue 2, information to be verified. Evaluate the quality of the reasoning rather than the name chosen.

12. Common mistakes

Do not present the size of the animal as a sufficient criterion. Do not announce cash for empty A-D cards. Do not confuse resemblance and identification.

  • Choose your favorite animal.
  • Deduce the species from a single curve.
  • Forget orientation and scale.
  • Use “certain” without reference.

13. Sources and limits

Zeder and Lapham (2010) show the limits of isolated morphological criteria for distinguishing certain postcranial bones of sheep and goats. A reliable attribution requires a reference corpus, an orientation, a scale and zooarchaeological validation. The bat remains an extension, outside the heart of the activity.

14. Current status and remaining validations

Corpus A-D not integrated. Species, photographs, metadata and final correction must be validated by a person competent in zooarchaeology. The interim scientific media documented in the redesign is explicitly excluded from these PDFs.

Useful phrases

Expected response

“The association is possible because I observe two clues, but the species must be verified with a reference corpus. »

Possible answer

Two groups can propose different animals if their clues are explained and their limits recognized.

Common errors

  • Confusing whole animal and shape of the bone.
  • Forget scale or orientation.
  • Taking a resemblance for identification.
  • Transform the method answer key into a species key.

Scientific limits

Bones A-D are not present in this version. So there is no correct association to reveal. This answer only evaluates the approach: observe, compare, argue, verify.

Complete only after archaeozoological validation of the corpus, the notes on each bone and the criteria that can really be used with students.
